Breath monitoring with earphones

Deep learning, audio · IEEE EMBC 2024 · PDF · arXiv · IEEE

Can a pair of ordinary wireless earphones tell whether someone is doing their breathing exercises correctly?

Problem

Deep breathing exercises are part of the treatment for many conditions, including asthma, COPD, high blood pressure, and anxiety. To know if a treatment is working, a care team needs to know if the patient is actually doing the exercises.

In a clinic, staff and special equipment can check this. At home, neither is available, so nobody really knows how well the exercises are being done.

What we built

A system that listens to breathing through the microphones of a pair of wireless earphones and reports, in real time, how the person is breathing. It looks at the audio in half-second pieces and passes each piece to two small convolutional neural networks:

  • a channel classifier that decides if the sound is nose breathing, mouth breathing, or a pause, and
  • a phase classifier that decides if it is a breath in or a breath out.

Together they give enough detail to check a breathing exercise step by step, for example "in through the nose, out through the mouth".

Why these choices

Earphones. Many people already own a pair, and they sit close to the nose and mouth. That makes them a practical sensor for home use, with no extra hardware to buy or learn.

Half-second windows. Short pieces of audio keep the delay low enough that feedback feels immediate while someone is doing the exercise.

Two networks instead of one. Nose versus mouth and in versus out are separate questions, so each network can focus on one and be judged on its own.

Mel-spectrograms. We compared two common ways of turning audio into model input. Mel-spectrograms scored higher than MFCCs for both networks, so we used them.

Result

With k-fold cross-validation, the channel classifier reached a best F1 score of 97.99% and the phase classifier reached 89.46%. The work was published at the IEEE Engineering in Medicine and Biology Conference (EMBC) in 2024.

It shows that cheap hardware people already have can support therapy at home. A next step is to combine this audio model with camera-based tracking to follow both movement and breathing exercises.
